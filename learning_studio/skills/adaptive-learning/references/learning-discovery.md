# Learning discovery

How to find out what a learner needs without making them fill in a form.

The rule is simple: **every dimension below is inferred first, asked second,
and asked one at a time.** A learner who has to answer sixteen questions before
seeing an exercise has already lost the session. Most dimensions never need a
question at all — the request, the conversation, the files in play, and the
learner's own writing carry most of the answers.

## The loop

1. **Infer.** Extract what the evidence already gives you.
2. **State it.** Put your assumption in one short line the learner can correct:
   "I'll aim at reading fluency rather than exam technique — say if that's
   wrong." Cheap, and it fails safely.
3. **Ask one question.** The single one whose answer most changes what you
   build next. Never a battery.
4. **Defer the rest.** Performance on the first exercises is better evidence
   than any opening question, and costs the learner nothing.
5. **Revise.** Treat every inference as provisional; update it the moment the
   learner's behaviour contradicts it.

## Which question is highest-value?

Ask the one whose two plausible answers would produce two genuinely different
exercises. If both answers lead you to build the same thing, do not ask it.

- Unknown **target level** usually beats unknown *current* level: it decides
  the ceiling, and the first exercises reveal the floor for free.
- Unknown **deadline** beats unknown preference: it decides coverage versus
  depth.
- Unknown **modality constraints** (no audio, screen reader, phone-only)
  outrank everything if getting them wrong makes the exercise unusable.

## The dimensions

**Subject.** What is being learned. Usually stated. Watch the scope: "French"
is not a subject, "the passé composé of irregular verbs" is workable.

**Goal.** What the learner wants to do differently. "Pass the exam", "read
papers unaided", "stop breaking production". Infer from urgency and framing.

**Success criteria.** How they would know they had arrived. Ask when the goal
is vague — this is the most commonly skipped and most valuable dimension.

**Current level.** Infer from their vocabulary and from what they get wrong.
Someone who writes "the pointer is dangling after the free" is not a beginner.
A short diagnostic beats a self-report; people misjudge their own level in both
directions.

**Target level.** Recognition, recall, application, or transfer? "Recognise
that a treaty ended the war" and "argue why it failed" are different projects.

**Prior knowledge.** What can you build on? Analogies to something they already
know are the cheapest teaching you have — recursion lands faster for someone
who knows mathematical induction.

**Knowledge gaps.** What is actually missing. Discovered through practice, not
questions. Distinguish a missing fact from a misconception: someone who thinks
photosynthesis happens in the mitochondrion has a wrong model, not a hole.

**Preferred modalities.** Text, audio, image, diagram, interaction. Infer from
what they engage with. Never treat a stated preference as a hard constraint on
what an objective requires: a listening goal needs audio regardless.

**Explanation language.** The language you teach and give feedback in.

**Content language.** The language of the material itself. These differ often
and deliberately — a Japanese reading drill explained in English. Keep both
explicit; conflating them produces incoherent items.

**Available time.** Per session and per week. It decides item count, not
ambition. Ten minutes a day is a real constraint, not a reason to refuse.

**Learning horizon.** Tonight's exam or next year's fluency. A short horizon
justifies cramming and coverage; a long one justifies spacing and depth. Say
out loud when the two conflict.

**Assessment preference.** Continuous low-stakes checks, occasional tests, or
no scoring at all. Some learners are demotivated by scores; ask if unsure.

**Feedback style.** Immediate or end-of-block. Terse or explanatory. Direct
correction or Socratic prompting. Ask once, then honour it consistently.

**Accessibility needs.** Screen reader, colour vision, motor precision,
dyslexia-friendly typography, no time pressure, captions. Never interrogate;
offer alternatives by default and let the learner take them. See
[accessibility](accessibility.md).

**Source material.** A textbook, lecture notes, a codebase, a syllabus. Always
ask whether one exists — practice drawn from the learner's own material beats
anything you invent, and it fixes terminology and notation for you.

**Learner constraints.** Age, exam board, curriculum, device, environment
(commuting, no audio), energy. These bound what is deliverable regardless of
what would be pedagogically ideal.

## One-off exercise or learning track?

This distinction decides whether anything durable is created, so get it right.

**A one-off** is the default. "Test me on this chapter", "explain this and quiz
me once". Serve it and stop. Do not build a curriculum, do not propose a
schedule unasked, and do not record preferences as durable.

**A track** is sustained work toward a goal over time. It exists only when the
learner has confirmed it explicitly — "yes, let's do this weekly until the
exam". Only then may goals and preferences be treated as durable, and only then
does the memory question in the main skill arise at all.

Signals that a one-off may be becoming a track: the learner returns to the same
subject, asks what to do next, or asks you to remember where they got to. Name
it and ask. Never promote a request into a track silently — it commits the
learner to something they did not choose.
